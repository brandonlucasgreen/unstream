# How to build your own music library without a streaming subscription

Streaming is convenient. Nobody's arguing that. But there's something it quietly took from us: the experience of *owning* music. Of building a collection that reflects your taste, that you can keep forever, and that actually puts money in artists' pockets.

If you've been thinking about reducing your dependency on Spotify or Apple Music, here's a practical guide to getting started — no cold-turkey required.

## Why bother?

Before the how, the why:

- **Save money long-term.** A streaming subscription runs $11–17/month ($132–204/year). Buying one album a month costs roughly the same but gives you something permanent.
- **Own your collection.** Cancel a streaming subscription and everything disappears. Purchased music is yours forever.
- **Curate your own taste.** Algorithms are designed to keep you engaged, not to broaden your horizons. Building your own library means *you* decide what's worth listening to.
- **Support artists directly.** On platforms like Bandcamp, 80–85% of your purchase goes to the artist. On Spotify, they might see $0.003 per stream.

## Step 1: Figure out what you actually listen to

Start with what you already know you love. Most streaming services let you see your listening history:

- **Spotify:** Check your "Top Artists" in Wrapped, or use a tool like [stats.fm](https://stats.fm) to see your all-time listening data
- **Apple Music:** Check your Replay playlists for yearly listening summaries
- **Last.fm / ListenBrainz:** If you scrobble, you already have detailed listening history

Make a short list of your top 15–20 artists. These are the ones you'll want to buy from first.

## Step 2: Start with your favorites

You don't need to replace your entire streaming library overnight. Start small:

- **Set a monthly music budget.** Even $10–15/month (roughly what you'd pay for streaming) goes a long way when you're buying albums.
- **Buy one or two albums a month** from artists you genuinely love. Prioritize the ones you listen to most.
- **Go for digital downloads** (FLAC or MP3) unless you're a vinyl collector. They're cheaper, instant, and play on everything.

Over the course of a year, you'll have 12–24 albums that are permanently yours — and you'll have put $100+ directly into artists' hands.

## Step 3: Find where your artists sell

This used to be the hard part. Independent artists sell their music across dozens of platforms — Bandcamp, Mirlo, Ampwall, their own websites, Ko-fi, and more. Checking each one individually is tedious.

[Unstream](https://unstream.stream) makes this simple: search for an artist and it shows you everywhere they sell or accept support across 23+ platforms, along with how much of your money they actually receive on each one. It's free and doesn't require an account.

## Step 4: Pick a music player

Once you have files, you need something to play them. Some options:

**Desktop:**
- **Apple Music / iTunes** — still works great as a local music library manager, even without a subscription
- **foobar2000** (Windows) — lightweight, highly customizable
- **Strawberry** (Linux/Mac/Windows) — open-source, full-featured

**Mobile:**
- **Apple Music** (iOS) — syncs local files from your Mac
- **Poweramp** (Android) — excellent local music player
- **Plexamp** — if you run a Plex server, this streams your own library to any device

**Self-hosted:**
- **Navidrome** or **Jellyfin** — run your own streaming server for your purchased music, accessible from anywhere

The key insight: you don't need a subscription service to have music on your phone. Local files work fine.

## Step 5: Use your library

Seriously — your public library likely offers free music access through **Hoopla** and **Freegal**. All you need is a library card.

These services let you stream and sometimes download music at no cost. The catalogs are surprisingly large. They're perfect for exploring new music without spending anything, and they support your local library system in the process.

## Step 6: Discover new music without algorithms

One of the best parts of leaving streaming behind is rediscovering how to find music on your own terms:

- **Friends and communities.** Ask people what they're listening to. Join music forums, subreddits, Discord servers, or Mastodon communities focused on music discovery.
- **Music blogs and publications.** Sites like Bandcamp Daily, The Quietus, and genre-specific blogs still do fantastic curation work.
- **Radio.** College radio, community radio, and internet radio stations (like NTS or KEXP) are goldmines for discovering artists you'd never find in an algorithm.
- **Live shows.** Nothing beats seeing an artist live and then buying their record at the merch table.
- **Artist recommendations.** Follow the artists you love on social media — they often share music from peers and influences.

## The hybrid approach

Here's the thing: you don't have to be all-or-nothing about this.

Many people keep a streaming subscription for casual listening and convenience, but *also* buy music directly from the artists they care about most. That's a perfectly valid approach. The goal isn't purity — it's shifting more of your music spending toward models that actually pay artists fairly.

Even replacing one streaming listen-session per week with time spent in your own library makes a difference. Over time, you might find that your owned collection grows large enough that you don't miss the subscription at all.

Start small. Buy one album this month from an artist you love. See how it feels. You might be surprised how much more connected you feel to the music when it's actually yours.
