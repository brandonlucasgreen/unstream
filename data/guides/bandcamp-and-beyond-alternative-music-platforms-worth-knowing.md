# Bandcamp and beyond: alternative music platforms worth knowing about

If you've been thinking about supporting artists more directly, you might already know about Bandcamp. But the landscape of artist-friendly music platforms is bigger — and more interesting — than most people realize.

Here's a tour of the platforms worth knowing about, organized by what they do and how they pay artists.

## Music marketplaces: buy and own

These platforms let you purchase music directly, usually as digital downloads (MP3, FLAC, WAV) and sometimes physical formats too.

### Bandcamp

The most well-known alternative. Bandcamp has been the go-to for independent musicians for over a decade, and for good reason: artists keep **80–85%** of every sale. On [Bandcamp Fridays](https://isitbandcampfriday.com) (the first Friday of each month), Bandcamp waives their cut entirely, pushing artist payouts to roughly **97%**.

Bandcamp offers digital downloads, vinyl and CD sales, merch, and a subscription model where fans can support artists monthly. Their catalog is massive, and chances are good that many of your favorite independent artists are already there.

### Mirlo

A **community-owned** music platform where artists keep **86–90%** of sales. Mirlo is built as a cooperative — the platform itself is governed by its community rather than venture capital. It's newer and smaller than Bandcamp, but growing steadily. If you care about the ownership structure behind the platforms you use, Mirlo is worth a look.

### Ampwall

One of the newer entrants, Ampwall offers artists an impressive **92–95%** payout on sales. It's focused on being a clean, simple storefront for independent musicians. Think of it as a modern, streamlined alternative to Bandcamp with even better economics for artists.

### Qobuz

Qobuz straddles the line between streaming and purchasing. You can buy individual albums in **hi-res audio quality** (up to 24-bit/192kHz), and artists receive roughly **70%** of sale prices. If you're an audiophile who cares about sound quality *and* artist compensation, Qobuz is the best of both worlds. They also offer a streaming tier, though the purchasing model is where they really differentiate.

## Decentralized and self-hosted

These platforms push the boundary further — giving artists full control over their storefronts, sometimes running on open protocols.

### Faircamp

[Faircamp](https://codeberg.org/simonrepp/faircamp) is an open-source tool that lets artists generate a **static website** to sell their music — essentially their own self-hosted Bandcamp. Because there's no platform middleman, artists keep **90–97%** of sales (only payment processor fees apply). The tradeoff is that artists need some technical comfort to set it up, but the [Faircamp Webring](https://faircamp.webr.ing) makes discovery easier.

### Bandwagon

An emerging platform built on [ActivityPub](https://activitypub.rocks/) — the same protocol that powers Mastodon and the broader fediverse. Bandwagon is still early, but it represents an exciting vision: a decentralized music marketplace where artists aren't locked into any single company's decisions. Worth watching as the fediverse grows.

## Patronage platforms: ongoing support

Not every way to support an artist involves buying a specific album. These platforms let fans provide ongoing financial support.

### Ko-fi

Artists keep an incredible **97% of tips** and **92% of sales** on Ko-fi. It's primarily a tipping and small-shop platform — great for one-time support or buying individual tracks/EPs. Many musicians use it alongside other platforms as a simple "buy me a coffee" link.

### Patreon

The original membership platform. Artists keep roughly **86–90%** of membership revenue on Patreon. It's best suited for artists who produce regular content — new tracks, behind-the-scenes updates, early access to releases. The subscription model gives artists a more predictable income stream than one-off sales.

### Buy Me a Coffee

Similar to Ko-fi but with a slightly different feel. Artists receive roughly **92%** of support. It's straightforward — fans can send one-time tips or set up monthly support. Many musicians use it as a simple, low-friction way to accept direct contributions.

## Community and collector platforms

### Jam.coop

A **cooperative music platform** that takes a radically different approach to streaming. Rather than the exploitative pro-rata model used by Spotify, Jam.coop is building a listener-owned co-op where subscription fees are distributed more fairly. It's small and still growing, but the model is compelling.

### Discogs

The largest **physical music marketplace** in the world. If you collect vinyl, CDs, or cassettes, Discogs is where you'll find almost anything. While it's primarily a secondary market (used records), many artists and labels also sell new releases directly through their Discogs shops.

## Library services: free and legal

Don't overlook your local library. Two services let you access music for free with just a library card:

### Hoopla

Hoopla offers on-demand music streaming and downloads through participating public libraries. You get a set number of borrows per month (typically 8–20, depending on your library). The catalog includes major and independent releases.

### Freegal

Similar to Hoopla, Freegal gives library cardholders access to streaming and limited downloads. Some libraries offer both services, giving you even more access.

While artist compensation through library services is unclear (and likely minimal), these are legitimate, free alternatives to paid streaming that support your local library system.

## Finding artists across all these platforms

The biggest challenge with this ecosystem is discovery. Your favorite artist might be on Bandcamp, Mirlo, *and* Ko-fi — but you'd never know unless you searched each platform individually.

This is exactly the problem [Unstream](https://unstream.stream) was built to solve. Search for any artist and it checks **23+ platforms** simultaneously, showing you everywhere they sell or accept support — along with how much of your money actually reaches them on each platform.

The alternative music platform ecosystem is thriving. The more people discover it, the more sustainable it becomes for the artists who depend on it.
