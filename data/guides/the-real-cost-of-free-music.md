# The real cost of "free" music

Every song ever recorded, on demand, for $10.99 a month (or free with ads). As a listener, it's hard to argue with that.

But the economics behind it are pretty grim if you're the one making the music. Here's how we got here.

## The short version of a long shift

**CDs and vinyl:** You bought a physical thing. A $15 CD might net an independent artist $5–10.

**iTunes and downloads (2003–2015):** You bought files. A $0.99 track or $9.99 album. Per-sale economics similar to physical — artists got a real cut. You owned the files.

**Streaming (2015–now):** You rent access to everything. Per-stream payouts dropped to **$0.003–0.005**. A fraction of a fraction of what a sale used to be worth.

Each step made music cheaper and more convenient for listeners. Each step also reduced what an individual artist could earn from a single fan.

## Who this works for

The streaming model isn't broken for everyone. Major labels — Universal, Sony, Warner — control 60–70% of the market and negotiated equity stakes in the platforms themselves. When Spotify's stock goes up, so does their portfolio. The pro-rata royalty model naturally funnels money toward their massive catalogs.

There's also the AI content farm problem. Thousands of AI-generated tracks get uploaded to streaming platforms to capture streams from algorithmic playlists — ambient stuff, lo-fi beats, background music. These cost almost nothing to produce and pull real money from the royalty pool that human artists share.

## Who it doesn't

An independent artist with 10,000 monthly listeners on Spotify — which is a decent number if you're building a career on your own — might earn **$30–50/month**. That doesn't cover a day of studio time.

Even mid-tier artists feel it. Musicians who could've sustained a career selling 50,000 albums now struggle to earn the equivalent from streaming, because the per-unit value collapsed. And organic discovery keeps getting harder as platforms optimize for engagement metrics and algorithmic curation rather than... the music being good.

## The alternative that already exists

On platforms like Bandcamp, Mirlo, Ampwall, and Faircamp, the economics are different:

- You pay for a specific thing — an album, a track, a membership
- The artist gets 80–97% of your payment, depending on the platform
- Your money goes to the artist you chose, not a pool divided by global market share
- You own what you buy

This isn't theoretical. Bandcamp alone has paid artists over a billion dollars. Mirlo is growing as a community-owned co-op. Faircamp lets artists run their own storefronts with near-zero fees.

## What to do about it

You don't have to quit streaming. But a few things go a long way:

1. **Pick the artists you care about most** and buy their music directly. Even one album purchase equals thousands of streams in artist income.
2. **Use [Unstream](https://unstream.stream)** to find where artists sell across 23+ platforms and see how much of your money reaches them on each one.
3. **Buy on Bandcamp Fridays** (first Friday of every month) when artists keep ~97%.
4. **Tell people.** Most listeners have no idea how any of this works.
